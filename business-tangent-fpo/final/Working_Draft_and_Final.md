**What this means for targets:** the base rate is "1 in 3 viable" and "1 in 8 above ₹1 crore". Any plan that promises all 12 FPOs viable and above ₹1 crore is promising something about 1 in 8 FPOs achieve today.

### A3. Market facts that shape the plan
- **Maize:** MSP ₹2,400 per quintal (2025-26), but Bihar doesn't procure maize through PACS, so farmers sell at market, often below MSP (S14). Small farmers sell in distress at harvest; larger ones hold 3 to 4 months (S15).
- **Ethanol is a buyer, not a guarantee.** The Purnia plant needs about 170 t a day. But the 2025-26 oil company tender cut Bihar's allocation below the 2022 contract and put cooperative sugar mills first, and plants ran below capacity for months (S18). Poultry feed is the other big buyer.
- **Vegetables:** losses of 4.9% to 11.6% after harvest (S12). A solar cold-room-as-a-service model already runs in Muzaffarpur (S8).
- **Floods** hit kharif crops in Kosi districts. Rabi maize is harvested in April and May, before the monsoon, so the flood risk for maize is mostly **stored stock**. Kharif vegetables are directly exposed.

## Part B. Draft 1 (first pass, as written before critique)

**Name:** Sell, Fund, Build.

**Priorities (all 12 FPOs):** 1) market access, 2) working capital, 3) management capacity.

**Interventions:**
1. **Buyer contracts.** Sign supply contracts with ethanol distilleries for maize and with retail chains for vegetables. Set up collection centres.
2. **Working capital grant.** Give each FPO ₹15 lakh to close the gap (₹1.8 crore for 12), and link FPOs to banks.

**Roadmap:** Year 1 set up, Year 2 scale, Year 3 exit.

**Budget (same every year):** market 25%, working capital 25%, infrastructure 30%, capacity 15%, monitoring 5%.

## Part C. Critique of Draft 1 from eight angles

**1. The judge (rubric lens).** Draft 1 doesn't show a single calculation, and the case asks for one ("show all relevant calculations or modelling"). Maize and vegetable FPOs get the same plan with a different building. Women and climate, which the note explicitly asks for, get one line each. *Fix:* a unit-economics model per FPO type, a maize vs vegetable column on every slide, and women and climate built into each intervention.

**2. The evidence (hit-rate lens).** "Every FPO at ₹1 crore by 2028" means all 12 jump into the top 12.7% within two years. "70% of members selling" is almost double what SRIJAN reached (39%) with a dedicated programme. *Fix:* build targets bottom-up and compare each with a benchmark. Year 5 turnover comes out at about ₹1.03 crore (3.8 times today). We target 6 of 12 FPOs (50%) fully covering costs against a national base rate of about 33%, and all 12 above 85%.

**3. The Foundation's finance head (value-for-money lens).** US$1 million a year over 12 FPOs is about ₹79 lakh per FPO a year, roughly three times their turnover. Most of Draft 1's money is spent once (grants, buildings) and leaves nothing that earns. There is no checkpoint at which money stops if results don't come. *Fix:* put money into capital that stays and earns (a revolving fund owned by the FPOs' federation). Taper salary support to zero. Add a Year 2 checkpoint. The model also shows the 12 FPOs can use only about ₹23 crore of ₹47.5 crore well. The honest answer is to use the rest to bring other Kosi FPOs into the same federation, and only after the checkpoint.

**4. The government (complementarity lens).** Draft 1 duplicates three schemes. It builds warehouses although Bihar has 6,158 new PACS godowns. It grants working capital, although the FPO's ₹10 lakh working capital is exactly its share capital (500 × ₹2,000), so it qualifies for a ₹10 to 13 lakh matching equity grant it hasn't claimed. It ignores AIF, MIDH and the e-NWR guarantee. Government CBBO support ends in 2027-28, so that's when the Foundation is most needed. *Fix:* claim before spending; lease before building; leverage before granting.

**5. The buyer (market lens).** Leaning on distilleries is risky: the 2025-26 tender cut Bihar's ethanol allocation and plants ran below capacity. A buyer needs consistent moisture (14% or lower), assured lot sizes and an invoice it can trust. Small FPOs can't offer 1,000 t lots alone. *Fix:* the three-buyer rule (no buyer above 50%): feed mills, distilleries and stored-stock sales via e-NWR. One federation sales desk sells the combined lots of 7 maize FPOs. For vegetables, plug into VEGFED and processors rather than compete with them.

**6. The farmer, especially the woman farmer (participation lens).** Why would a farmer sell to the FPO instead of the village trader who pays cash on the spot and lends for seed? Draft 1 has no answer. Board seats without a pipeline of trained women are tokenism. *Fix:* payment within 7 days from the revolving fund, input credit repaid in produce, honest weighing and moisture testing. For women: enrol 150 women per FPO through JEEViKA self-help groups, 2 paid women procurement agents per FPO, a leadership fellowship that prepares women to stand for board seats, and crèches at collection points. Women-led FPOs survive better (S10), so this is a business case, not only a social one.

**7. The flood (climate lens).** Draft 1 builds new warehouses in flood-prone districts. In Kosi the maize crop is safe (harvested before the monsoon) but stored stock is not. Vegetables lose a kharif season in a bad flood year. The model shows a flood year takes a vegetable FPO from 92% to 74% cost cover. *Fix:* store in raised PACS godowns above flood level, build pack sheds on raised plinths, keep a shade-net nursery so seedlings can be replanted fast, run a flood emergency loan window inside the fund, issue flood alerts, and help farmers file state crop-aid claims (Bihar has no PMFBY).

**8. The devil's advocate (sustainability lens).** "After support, FPOs cover their costs" is the whole point of the case, and Draft 1 doesn't prove it. The model shows why it's hard: today an FPO keeps about ₹1.4 lakh a year against ₹5.9 to 6.3 lakh to run professionally (about 22% cover). A single 500-member FPO can't afford its own accountant, auditor and sales team. *Fix:* a federation of KAP's FPOs that shares those costs and pays for itself from fees, a 1% commission on bulk maize, bulk input buying and interest on the revolving fund. The model has it covering 158% of its running cost by Year 5.

## Part D. Final draft (6 slides plus appendix)

**Strategy name: Sell together, Fund the season, Store smart.** Run through a Kosi FPO federation that outlives KAP.

### Slide 1. The answer and how we'll know it worked

**Headline:** Today a KAP FPO earns about 22% of what it costs to run professionally. Our plan lifts that to 92% to 98% by 2030-31 and builds a federation that keeps services running after grants end.

| Test (end of Year 5, 2030-31) | Today | Target | Benchmark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average turnover per FPO | ₹27.5 lakh | ₹1.03 crore | 12.7% of scheme FPOs exceed ₹1 crore (S3) |
| FPOs covering 100% of running cost for 2 years | 0 of 12 | 6 of 12; all 12 above 85% | About 1 in 3 nationally (S1) |
| Members selling through the FPO | About 15% (our estimate) | 48% | SRIJAN 39% after 2 years (S9) |
| Women members / women directors | 20% / 1 of 9 | 38% / 3 of 9 | Women-led FPOs survive 4 to 13 points more often (S10) |
| FPOs with their own bank or NBFC credit line | 0 | 12 | Only 30% of FPOs have one (S4) |
| Federation covers its own running cost | No federation | Yes (158% in model) | |

### Slide 2. Strategic priorities (Deliverable 1)

**Headline:** Volume is the root problem. Too little produce flows through the FPO to pay a CEO, so we prioritise selling together and funding the season, then build the people and systems to run it.

**Diagnosis (per FPO, ₹ lakh a year):** income kept today about 1.4 (5% of ₹27.5 lakh) against a professional running cost of 5.9 (maize) to 6.3 (vegetables). Working capital ₹10 lakh equals share capital (500 × ₹2,000). Nothing else funds the season, against a ₹25 lakh peak.

| Priority | Why it matters most | Common to all 12 | Maize FPOs (7) | Vegetable FPOs (5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Market access and volume | Margin per rupee is thin, so only volume pays fixed costs | Written buyer agreements, honest weighing, payment within 7 days | Bulk lots of 1,000 t+ to feed mills and distilleries; moisture at 14% or lower; hold part of the crop to sell later | Daily small lots, perishable; grading, crates, cold rooms; VEGFED and processors |
| 2. Seasonal finance | ₹15 lakh gap today; buyers pay in 30 to 60 days | Claim matching equity grant; revolving fund; bank line | One big rabi buying season (April to June); stored stock financed by e-NWR pledge loans | Faster cycle; smaller, year-round need |
| 3. Management capacity and member participation (incl. women) | No accounts or compliance means no bank loan and no exit | Shared accountant and compliance via the federation; CEO paid from margin by Year 5 | | |

**Why not infrastructure as a separate priority:** it's needed, but it only pays where it adds sales (drying for maize, cold rooms for vegetables). So it sits inside the market intervention as an enabler.

### Slide 3. Three interventions and how KAP runs them (Deliverable 2)

**Headline:** Three interventions, each built to leave something that keeps earning when the grant stops.

**1. Sell together.** A federation sales desk (KAP staff in Years 1 to 3, federation staff from Year 4).
- *Maize:* combine the 7 FPOs into lots of 1,000 t or more. Three-buyer rule: feed mills, distilleries, and stored-stock sales through e-NWR; no buyer above 50%. Drying yard, moisture meter, scale and a shared mobile dryer per FPO (₹10 lakh kit).
- *Vegetables:* 3 village collection points, 2,000 crates, a pack shed on a raised plinth, two 5 t solar cold rooms, a shade-net nursery (₹40 lakh kit; MIDH pays 35%). Sell to VEGFED, processors and city traders.
- *Women:* 2 paid women procurement agents per FPO, recruited from JEEViKA self-help groups.

**2. Fund the season.**
- Step 1: clean share registers and claim the matching equity grant (₹13 lakh per FPO at 650 members). Free money, the biggest single fix.
- Step 2: a revolving fund owned by the federation (₹2 crore plus a ₹0.5 crore flood window). It lends to FPOs at 10%, below the 14% to 15% NBFC rate. Uses: pay farmers within 7 days, and give members input credit repaid in produce.
- Step 3: a ₹0.5 crore first-loss deposit with a partner bank, plus the NABARD credit guarantee, to open bank lines in each FPO's own name.
- *Maize:* stored stock financed by e-NWR pledge loans (CGS-NPF guarantee), so storage gains don't tie up the fund.

**3. Run it professionally, together.**
- A federation of KAP's FPOs (a producer company whose members are the FPOs) that provides accounts, audit, filings, a market desk and bulk input buying for ₹1.2 lakh a year per FPO.
- CEO and assistant salary support tapering 80%, 60%, 40%, 20%, 0%.
- Accounting software, compliance clean-up, board training.
- Women: leadership fellowship, crèches, 150 new women members per FPO through self-help groups.
- Climate: flood alerts, resilient seed trials, help with state crop-aid claims, a flood emergency loan window.

### Slide 4. Expected outcomes and assumptions (Deliverable 2, continued)

**Headline:** By Year 5 a typical FPO handles 3.8 times today's business and covers 92% to 98% of a fully professional running cost. Closing the last few percent is a stretch target we track, not a promise.

| Per FPO, Year 5 (₹ lakh a year) | Maize | Vegetables |
|---|---|---|
| Members (incl. 150 new women) | 650 | 650 |
| Members selling through FPO | 50% (325) | 45% (293) |
| Produce handled | 342 t | 559 t |
| Produce sales | 77.0 | 78.3 |
| Input and nursery sales | 27.3 | 23.2 |
| **Turnover** | **104.3** | **101.5** |
| Income kept (margins, storage gain, service fees) | 7.8 | 9.4 |
| Running cost (CEO, assistant, office, federation fee, upkeep, loan repayment) | 8.0 | 10.2 |
| **Share of running cost covered** | **98%** | **92%** |

**Key assumptions (all in the model with sources):** 0.4 ha maize at 4.5 t/ha per selling member (Bihar about 4 t/ha, rabi higher); vegetables 0.2 ha at 22.5 t/ha a year; FPO handles 65% (maize) and 50% (vegetables) of a seller's crop; maize ₹2,250 per quintal, vegetables ₹14/kg; margins 3% on maize, 7% on vegetables, 8% to 10% on inputs; 40% of maize stored for a net 4% gain.

**Sensitivity (cost cover, maize / vegetables):** base 98% / 92%; only 39% of members sell (SRIJAN level) 82% / 85%; prices 10% lower 91% / 87%; maize yield 4.0 t/ha 90% / 92%; flood year costs a vegetable season 98% / 74%.

**What farmers get:** a better price and honest weighing at the FPO, cheaper inputs, and the option to hold maize for a later sale. Bihar's JEEViKA maize company delivered 15% to 20% higher returns (S19). We plan conservatively below that.

### Slide 5. Roadmap and exit (Deliverable 3)

**Headline:** KAP builds in Years 1 and 2, hands over to the federation and FPOs in Years 3 and 4, and steps back in Year 5. Money continues only if a Year 2 checkpoint is passed.

| Phase | Main activities | Milestones | Indicators tracked |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Year 1 (2026-27): Fix the base** | Clean share registers; claim matching equity grant; Tally accounts; asset kits (60%); revolving fund opens; first buyer agreements; women enrolment starts; 6 orphaned Kosi FPOs join (cohort 2) | 12 of 12 filings on time; grant claimed; 3+ written buyer agreements | Members selling, payment days, fund repayment rate |
| **Year 2 (2027-28): Scale and set up the federation** | Federation registered; e-NWR live in leased PACS godowns; remaining kits; flood window opens; government CBBO support ends | 30% of members selling; repayment 95% or more | Turnover per FPO, cost cover, women agents active |
| **Checkpoint (end of Year 2)** | Release Years 3 to 5 money and 18 more cohort 2 FPOs only if: 9 of 12 FPOs above 50% cost cover, fund repayment 95% or more, 2+ maize buyers in use, women members 28% or more | | |
| **Years 3 to 4: FPOs run, federation serves** | Salary support 40%, then 20%; sales desk moves to federation payroll; KAP becomes an adviser | Bank lines in each FPO's own name; federation covers 75%+ of its cost | Cost cover, buyer concentration, women directors |
| **Year 5 (2030-31): Graduate** | No salary support; KAP exits cohort 1; Foundation money only for cohort 2's taper | Graduation tests met | Same tests, audited |

**Who does what, Year 1 vs Year 5:**

| Function | Year 1 | Year 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Finding and negotiating with buyers | KAP | Federation sales desk |
| Accounts, audit, filings | KAP | Federation shared team |
| Buying from farmers, payment | FPO with KAP | FPO CEO and women agents |
| Lending decisions | KAP with Foundation | Federation credit committee with partner bank |
| Governance | KAP trains the board | FPO board (3 of 9 women) |

**Conditions an FPO must meet before support ends (two years in a row):**
1. Covers 100% of running cost from its own income.
2. At least 40% of members sell through it.
3. At least 2 buyers with written agreements, none above 50% of sales.
4. Credit line in its own name, nothing overdue beyond 90 days.
5. Audited accounts and statutory filings on time.
6. At least 3 women directors and 30% women members.
7. A flood plan tested in the last season.

**Honest weaknesses that remain (judges may push on these):**
1. **The second cohort is half the budget.** A judge may say the case is about KAP's 12 FPOs. Our defence: the 12 can't usefully absorb more than about US$2.4 million, and forcing it in creates dependence. If the Foundation prefers, the fallback is a 3-year commitment focused on the 12, with the cohort 2 money not committed.
2. **Even the base case doesn't reach 100% cost cover on average.** We say so openly; graduation needs the better half of FPOs to beat the average.
3. **Some numbers are assumptions, not data:** current selling share (15%), vegetable yield and price, input spend, margins, storage gain. Each is labelled.
4. **Benchmarks were confirmed through search results, not on the original pages.** This cloud session's network blocks most government and news sites. Before submission, open S1, S2, S3, S4, S9, S10 and S16 on your laptop and check the exact wording.
5. **Legal check:** a producer company federation lending to its member FPOs should be confirmed with a company secretary. Fallback: run the fund through a partner NBFC with the Foundation's money as first loss.
6. **Farmer income gains per head are modest** (a few thousand rupees a year per selling member in our conservative case). The case's goal is FPO viability, but a judge focused on impact may ask.
